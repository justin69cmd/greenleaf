"Eat the rainbow" sounds like something off a cereal box, but it's a surprisingly good shortcut. The pigments that give fruits and vegetables their colors are often the same compounds — the phytonutrients — that make them worth eating. So a plate with a range of colors tends, without any calorie counting, to cover a wide range of nutrients.

## The colors are a rough nutrient map

Each color family clusters around certain beneficial compounds:

- **Red** (tomatoes, red peppers, strawberries) — often rich in lycopene and vitamin C.
- **Orange and yellow** (carrots, squash, citrus) — carotenoids the body can turn into vitamin A, plus more vitamin C.
- **Green** (spinach, broccoli, kale) — folate, vitamin K, and a range of protective plant compounds.
- **Blue and purple** (blueberries, red cabbage, eggplant) — anthocyanins, the antioxidants behind those deep hues.
- **White and brown** (garlic, onions, mushrooms) — their own useful compounds, so don't skip the "colorless" ones.

You don't need to memorize this. The point is that variety of color is a stand-in for variety of nutrients, and variety is the part most of us actually miss.

> Aim for a few different colors across the day, not a rainbow in every single meal. It's the pattern over a week that matters.

## Why variety beats chasing a single "superfood"

Every year a new berry or seed gets crowned a superfood. The honest picture is duller and more freeing: no single food carries the day, and the benefits come from eating a broad mix of plants regularly. Kale is great; kale every day to the exclusion of everything else is just a narrower diet. Spread your bets across colors and types.

## Frozen counts. So do canned and dried.

Fresh produce is lovely, but "I didn't have fresh spinach" is a bad reason to eat no vegetables. Frozen vegetables are picked and frozen at peak ripeness and are nutritionally excellent. Canned beans, no-salt-added canned tomatoes, and dried lentils are cheap, keep for ages, and count fully. The best vegetable is the one you'll actually eat this week.

## Practical ways to add a color

- Toss a handful of frozen berries into oatmeal or yogurt.
- Keep pre-washed greens on hand and add a fistful to eggs, pasta, or a sandwich.
- Roast a tray of mixed vegetables once and eat from it for two or three days.
- Make half your plate vegetables before you plate anything else — it quietly crowds out less.

## Keep the goal boringly achievable

If your current baseline is "not many vegetables," don't aim for a perfect, photogenic plate. Aim for *one more color than yesterday*. Small, repeatable additions build into a genuinely varied diet over months — which is the timescale that matters.

This is general nutrition information, not personalized dietary advice; specific conditions or restrictions are worth discussing with a doctor or registered dietitian. But for most people, "add more colors, more often" is advice that's hard to get wrong.
