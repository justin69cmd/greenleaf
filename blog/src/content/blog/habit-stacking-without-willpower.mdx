Most failed routines fail the same way: they depend on remembering to do the thing and mustering the motivation to do it. Both of those run out. Habit stacking sidesteps the problem by hooking a new habit onto one you already do automatically — so the old habit becomes the reminder, and the new one rides its momentum.

## The formula

The core pattern is one sentence:

> After I [existing habit], I will [new habit].

For example: *After I pour my morning coffee, I will write down my top task for the day.* Or: *After I sit down at my desk, I will do five minutes of slow breathing.* The existing habit is already wired in and cue-triggered; you're borrowing that trigger instead of trying to build a new one from scratch.

## Why it beats "just be disciplined"

A brand-new habit has no natural cue, so you rely on memory and willpower to launch it — and those are exactly the resources that vanish when you're tired, busy, or stressed. An established habit, by contrast, happens without a decision. By anchoring to it, the new behavior inherits a reliable starting gun. You're not adding a habit so much as extending one.

## Make the new habit tiny at first

The most common mistake is stacking something too big. *After I sit down at my desk, I will work out for an hour* won't survive contact with a real Tuesday. Shrink the new habit until it's almost trivially easy — five minutes, one page, one set. Consistency compounds; a small habit done daily beats a big one done sporadically, and it can always grow later once it's automatic.

## Choose the anchor carefully

A good anchor is:

- **Something you already do without fail** — a meal, brushing your teeth, making coffee, closing your laptop.
- **At the right time of day** for the new habit (don't stack "go for a run" onto "get into bed").
- **Specific.** "After lunch" is vaguer than "after I put my lunch plate in the sink." The more concrete the cue, the more reliably it fires.

## Stack in the right order, then chain

Once one stack is solid, you can chain another onto it, building a small sequence: coffee → write top task → five minutes of breathing → start work. But add links one at a time. Trying to install a five-step morning routine overnight is just willpower in disguise, and it breaks the same way.

## Expect misses, and design for them

You will skip days — a late start, a sick kid, a trip. That's normal and not a reason to abandon the habit. The rule that keeps routines alive is simply *never miss twice*. One miss is an accident; two starts to become the new pattern. Get back to the stack the very next opportunity and the habit survives.

Behavior change is slow and personal, and what anchors well for one person won't for another. But the underlying move — stop relying on motivation, start relying on cues you already have — is one of the most dependable tools there is for making a routine that lasts.
