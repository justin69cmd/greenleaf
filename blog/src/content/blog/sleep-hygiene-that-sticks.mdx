Good sleep is less about a single perfect night and more about a few reliable signals you send your body every day. The trouble with most "sleep hygiene" lists is that they hand you fifteen rules at once — and fifteen new habits is fourteen too many. This guide focuses on the small set of changes that carry the most weight, and how to make them stick.

## Start with a consistent wake time, not bedtime

It feels backwards, but the anchor of a healthy sleep schedule is when you *get up*, not when you go to bed. Your circadian rhythm — the roughly 24-hour internal clock that governs alertness and sleepiness — takes its strongest cue from morning light. A steady wake time (yes, including weekends, within an hour) gradually pulls your natural sleepiness earlier in the evening on its own.

> Pick a wake time you can keep on your hardest day, not your easiest one. A schedule you follow 6 days a week beats a perfect one you abandon by Wednesday.

## Give light the job it's meant to do

Light is the master switch. In the first hour after waking, get bright light — ideally sunlight through a window or a short walk outside. In the last hour before bed, do the opposite: dim the room and cut the brightest screens. You don't need to banish your phone entirely; lowering overall light exposure matters more than any single device.

## Build a 30-minute "landing strip"

You can't slam from a full day into sleep. Create a short, repeatable wind-down — the same three or four low-stimulation things in roughly the same order each night. It might be: dishes done, lights down, a few pages of a paperback, then bed. The *sequence* is the signal. After a couple of weeks your brain starts treating step one as the on-ramp to sleep.

## Protect the bed's one association

If you scroll, work, and worry in bed, your brain learns that bed is a place for being alert. Keep the bed for sleep. If you're wide awake after about 20 minutes, get up, sit somewhere dim and dull for a bit, and return when you feel sleepy. This sounds fussy, but it's one of the most evidence-backed pieces of the whole picture.

## The inputs that quietly wreck sleep

Three common ones are worth naming:

- **Caffeine's long tail.** It can linger for many hours; an afternoon coffee is an evening sleep problem for a lot of people. Try moving your cutoff earlier for a week and notice the difference.
- **Alcohol as a "nightcap."** It may help you fall asleep but fragments the second half of the night, so you wake unrefreshed.
- **The 3 p.m. energy crash nap.** A short early-afternoon rest is fine; a long or late nap borrows from tonight's sleep.

## Make it stick: change one thing at a time

The reason sleep advice bounces off is that people try to overhaul everything on Sunday night and burn out by Tuesday. Instead, pick **one** change — say, a fixed wake time — and run it for two weeks until it's automatic. Then add the next. Slower is faster here, because the habits you keep are the only ones that count.

None of this is medical advice, and persistent insomnia or loud snoring with daytime exhaustion is worth raising with a clinician. But for the ordinary "I just don't sleep as well as I'd like," these few levers, applied patiently, do most of the work.
