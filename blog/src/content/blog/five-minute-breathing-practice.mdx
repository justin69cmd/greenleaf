When you're stressed, most advice is hard to follow precisely *because* you're stressed. Breathing is the exception. It's the one part of your stress response you can consciously steer, and steering it sends a signal the rest of your body listens to.

## Why slow breathing works

Your breath is wired to your autonomic nervous system — the automatic controls behind heart rate, digestion, and the fight-or-flight response. Fast, shallow, upper-chest breathing tends to accompany stress. Slow breathing, especially with a longer exhale than inhale, nudges the system the other way, toward "rest and digest." You're not tricking yourself calm; you're using a real physiological lever.

> The exhale is the important half. A long, unhurried out-breath is what tips the balance toward calm.

## The practice: 4 in, 6 out

Here's a version simple enough to remember when you're rattled:

1. **Sit or stand comfortably.** Let your shoulders drop. You don't need to close your eyes.
2. **Breathe in through your nose for a count of 4**, letting your belly expand rather than your chest rising.
3. **Breathe out slowly through your nose or mouth for a count of 6.** Make the exhale smooth, not forced.
4. **Repeat for about five minutes** — roughly 25–30 breaths. If counting to 6 feels strained, use 3 in and 5 out. The ratio matters more than the exact numbers.

That's the whole thing. No app, no special posture, no incense.

## When to use it

The practice is most powerful *before* you need it — a daily five minutes builds a baseline of calm that's easier to return to. But it also works in the moment: before a hard conversation, in the minutes after bad news, when you notice your jaw is clenched, or as a bridge into sleep.

## If your mind wanders (it will)

Wandering attention isn't failure — it's what minds do. Each time you notice you've drifted into planning or replaying something, that noticing *is* the practice. Gently bring your attention back to the count. Over time, the "coming back" gets quicker and the drift gets shorter.

## Keep it small enough to repeat

A five-minute practice you do most days beats a 30-minute one you do twice and quit. Attach it to something you already do — the first five minutes after you sit at your desk, or right after you brush your teeth — so it rides an existing habit instead of demanding a new slot in your day.

If you live with an anxiety condition or panic attacks, breathwork can be a genuinely useful tool, but it works best alongside support from a professional rather than instead of it. For everyday stress, though, this is about as close as it gets to a free, always-available reset.
